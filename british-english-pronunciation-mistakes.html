Pronunciation guide

Common British English pronunciation mistakes.

You do not need to lose your accent. Focus on the pronunciation features that affect clarity, rhythm and how easily people understand you.

Pronunciation problems are rarely about one sound in isolation. Stress, weak vowels, endings and rhythm often matter just as much as individual consonants. These are some of the most useful areas to check if your English is grammatically strong but still feels difficult to understand.

1. Putting stress on the wrong syllable

English word stress is not always predictable. A word can be pronounced with the correct sounds but still be hard to recognise if the stress is in the wrong place.

Examples:
STRAT-e-gy — stress on the first syllable.
con-FI-dent — stress on the second syllable.
AC-tion-a-ble — stress begins on the first syllable.
Practice: mark the stressed syllable in new vocabulary rather than writing only the spelling.

2. Pronouncing every vowel strongly

British English uses many weak vowel sounds, especially the schwa /ə/. If every syllable is pronounced with equal strength, speech can sound heavy and unnatural.

Example: “support” is not usually pronounced with two equally strong vowels. The first syllable is weak: /səˈpɔːt/.

Listen for weak syllables in words such as about, support, problem, information and development.

3. Mixing up /θ/ and /ð/

English has two common TH sounds:

  • /θ/ in think, method, three
  • /ð/ in this, those, other
Practice contrast: think – this · three – these · method – mother.

The tongue should lightly touch or come between the teeth. Replacing TH with /s/, /z/, /t/ or /d/ can sometimes change the word enough to affect understanding.

4. Losing final consonants

Final sounds carry important grammar and meaning. If the ending disappears, listeners may miss plurals, tense or even the word itself.

Compare: work / worked · need / needs · friend / friends · develop / developed.

Do not exaggerate the final sound, but make sure it is present.

5. Treating all -ed endings the same

Regular past-tense endings are commonly pronounced in three ways:

  • /t/ — worked, watched, helped
  • /d/ — played, cleaned, called
  • /ɪd/ — wanted, needed, decided
Useful rule: /ɪd/ is normally used after /t/ or /d/. Do not add a full extra syllable to every -ed verb.

6. Overpronouncing plural -s

Plural and third-person -s endings also vary:

  • /s/ — books, stops
  • /z/ — friends, lives
  • /ɪz/ — places, watches, choices

Practising these endings improves both pronunciation and grammatical clarity.

7. Stressing every word in a sentence

English rhythm depends on stressed content words and weaker grammar words.

Natural focus: “I NEED to FINISH the REPORT by FRIDAY.”

Words such as to, the, by are normally weaker. Equal stress on every word makes speech sound less natural and can hide the important information.

8. Speaking word by word

Fluent speech connects words together. You do not need to copy every feature of fast native speech, but some linking makes your English easier to follow.

“pick it up” → the consonants and vowels connect.
“an important meeting” → words flow rather than stopping after each one.

9. Confusing long and short vowels

Some vowel contrasts can change meaning. For many learners, contrasts such as /ɪ/ and /iː/ need focused practice.

Examples: ship / sheep · live / leave · fill / feel.

The goal is not dramatic exaggeration. It is maintaining enough contrast for the listener to identify the word easily.

10. Adding extra vowels inside consonant clusters

Words with several consonants together can be difficult. Learners sometimes insert an extra vowel, which changes the rhythm.

Examples to practise slowly: asked · worked · strengths · scripts · months.

Build the word gradually rather than trying to say it at full speed immediately.

11. Copying spelling instead of sound

English spelling is unreliable. Words such as walk, worked, comfortable, business and fruit are often mispronounced because learners pronounce every written letter.

Use audio and phonemic information for words that repeatedly cause problems instead of relying only on spelling.

12. Trying to “sound British” before becoming clear

A British accent is not the main goal. Clarity comes first. Improving stress, rhythm, consonant endings and your personal problem sounds will usually make a much bigger difference than copying accent features.

Simple weekly routine:
1. Choose five difficult words from your real work or lessons.
2. Mark stress.
3. Listen to a reliable model.
4. Record each word in a full sentence.
5. Compare your version and repeat.
