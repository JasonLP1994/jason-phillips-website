Pronunciation guide

British English pronunciation: improve clarity, stress and rhythm.

You do not need to lose your accent to speak excellent English. Focus on the features that make you easier to understand: stress, rhythm, connected speech and the sounds that matter most.

By Jason Phillips, British English teacher · Updated 4 October 2026

Pronunciation improvement is often misunderstood. Learners sometimes try to copy every tiny feature of a native accent, but that is rarely the best use of time. A clearer goal is intelligibility: can people understand you easily, especially when you speak at normal speed?

For most learners, the biggest gains come from a combination of individual sounds and the rhythm of whole sentences.

1. Word stress matters as much as individual sounds

English words usually have one syllable that is stronger than the others. If the stress falls in the wrong place, even a correctly pronounced word can be difficult to recognise.

STRAt-e-gicPractise the stressed syllable clearly, then reduce the weaker syllables.
CON-fi-dentDo not give every syllable equal weight.
AC-tion-a-bleKeep the main stress strong and the middle syllables lighter.

When learning new vocabulary, learn the stress pattern at the same time as the meaning.

2. Sentence stress creates natural rhythm

English is stress-timed. Important words usually receive more emphasis, while grammar words are often shorter and weaker.

“I SENT the REPORT on MONday.”The key information carries the stress.
“We NEED to CHANGE the DEADline.”The stressed words carry the message.

If you stress every word equally, your speech can sound slow and mechanical. If you remove too much stress, important information can become unclear.

3. The schwa /ə/ is one of the most useful sounds in English

The schwa is the weak vowel sound found in many unstressed syllables. It appears constantly in natural British English.

  • about → the first vowel is weak
  • support → the first syllable is reduced
  • problem → the final syllable is often weak
  • professional → several unstressed syllables reduce

Trying to pronounce every written vowel fully often makes English sound overly careful. Learning where vowels reduce can improve both rhythm and listening comprehension.

4. Connected speech: words do not live separately

In real speech, words connect. Final consonants can link into the next vowel, sounds can become weaker, and common combinations are produced as one rhythm unit.

“pick it up”Practise the phrase as one connected chunk rather than three separate words.
“an important issue”Link the final consonant into the following vowel where natural.
“want to” / “going to”In informal speech, function words are often reduced. Keep clarity appropriate to the setting.
Professional context: You do not need extremely casual reductions in a presentation or interview. The goal is natural, clear connected speech rather than slang for its own sake.

5. The TH sounds

English has two common TH sounds: the unvoiced sound in think and the voiced sound in this. For both, the tongue comes lightly between the teeth.

  • think, three, method, something
  • this, those, other, together

Practise the sound in complete phrases, not only isolated words: “three different methods”, “those other options”, “I think this works”.

6. S, Z, SH and CH

These sounds can create misunderstandings because they are close in the mouth but carry different meanings.

  • /s/: see, process, place
  • /z/: zero, reason, present
  • /ʃ/: she, pressure, professional
  • /tʃ/: change, choice, approach

Use minimal pairs and short phrases, then move quickly into normal sentences.

7. Final consonants and grammatical endings

Dropping final sounds can affect meaning, particularly with plurals, third-person verbs and past tense endings.

  • work / worked
  • need / needs
  • method / methods
  • recipe / recipes

The goal is not to exaggerate the ending. Make it audible enough that the listener receives the grammatical information.

8. The -ed ending has three common pronunciations

  • /t/: worked, watched
  • /d/: played, changed
  • /ɪd/: wanted, needed

Practise the ending as part of the whole word. Adding a full extra syllable to every -ed word can make speech sound unnatural.

Practise the three ED endings with examples, syllable tips and a six word challenge.

9. Intonation changes meaning and attitude

Intonation is the movement of your voice. It helps listeners understand whether you are finishing a point, checking information, showing contrast or keeping the floor.

In professional communication, intonation also affects how confident, engaged or diplomatic you sound. A sentence can be grammatically perfect but still sound uncertain if your voice rises in the wrong place.

10. A practical pronunciation routine

  1. Choose one target sound or stress pattern.
  2. Practise five words containing it.
  3. Put those words into short phrases.
  4. Record one 30-second answer using the phrases naturally.
  5. Listen back once for clarity, not perfection.
  6. Repeat the answer with one specific improvement.

Pronunciation becomes more reliable when you practise it inside the kind of English you actually use — meetings, presentations, interviews or everyday conversation.
