In, on or at? The simple guide to time and place.

These three tiny words cause a huge amount of confusion. The good news is that the system becomes much easier once you stop memorising random examples and start seeing the pattern.

By Jason Phillips · British English Coach · Published 12 October 2026 · 6 min read
The quick rule:
At is usually a point. On is usually a surface, day or date. In is usually an enclosed space or a longer period.

You're arranging lunch with a colleague. Do you meet in the station, on the station or at the station? And is the meeting in Monday or on Monday? These tiny words carry a surprising amount of meaning. The good news is that you don't need to memorise a hundred disconnected rules to start making better choices.

Use “at” for a point

Use at when you are thinking about one exact point in time or one specific point in a place.

at 9:00

at lunchtime

at the station

at the entrance

at work

For British English, at the weekend is also very common.

Use “on” for days, dates and surfaces

Use on with days and dates. For places, use it when something is physically on a surface or when English treats the location as a line or public transport surface.

on Monday

on 15 October

on the table

on the wall

on the bus

Use “in” for enclosed spaces and longer periods

Use in with months, years, seasons and longer periods. For places, use it when you think of the place as an area or space that contains you.

in October

in 2026

in the morning

in London

in the room

One useful contrast

I will meet you at the airport.

I am waiting in the airport.

The first sentence treats the airport as a meeting point. The second treats it as a building you are inside. Both are correct, but the idea is different.

Common mistakes

I have a meeting in Monday.

I have a meeting on Monday.

The call starts on 9:00.

The call starts at 9:00.

I was born on 1994.

I was born in 1994.

FOLLOW THE JOURNEY

One morning, three little prepositions

“I'm meeting a colleague at the station on Monday. We'll travel on the train and arrive in London before lunch.”

Notice how naturally the words work together: at marks the meeting point, on identifies the day or form of public transport, and in places you within the city. If you're driving, you would usually say “in the car” instead. That is why examples are more useful than trying to force every expression into one universal rule.

Professional English examples

These prepositions appear constantly in schedules, emails and meetings.

The presentation starts at 10:30.

We will send the report on Friday.

The conference takes place in November.

Let us meet at reception.

The figures are shown on page 12.

Quick practice

1. The meeting is ___ Tuesday.

2. I will call you ___ 3:00.

3. We moved here ___ 2024.

4. The document is ___ my desk.

5. She is waiting ___ reception.

Answers: on, at, in, on, at.

The takeaway

Think in terms of size and focus. At is a point. On is a day, date or surface. In is a larger space or longer period. It will not explain every fixed expression in English, but it gives you a strong rule for the majority of everyday situations.
