Give young learners a reason to use their English.

How I connect a student's interests with focused practice and feedback.

By Jason Phillips · 2 October 2026 · 3 min read
A young learner can finish every exercise correctly and still struggle to use the English in a conversation.
That's something I pay attention to when planning my lessons.

Find out what they can do independently

Can they use the vocabulary without a word bank? Can they explain why they think something? Can they build their own sentence when the example disappears?

Their answers help me decide what we need to practise next.

After 13+ years teaching in England, Thailand and Vietnam, including building and running my own language centre in Da Nang, I've learnt how important it is to connect that practice to something the student cares about.

Use their interests to give the language a purpose

If they enjoy gaming, we can practise giving instructions. If they love sport, we can compare players and explain opinions. If they're interested in technology, we can discuss an invention and how it works.

The interest gets them involved. The teaching gives the activity direction.

For example, a game can create opportunities to ask questions or explain a choice. I can then focus the feedback on a particular skill—such as forming a question, giving a reason or making a sentence clearer.

Listen for the next step

I use games and challenges in my lessons, but I'm also listening carefully: where do they hesitate, which mistakes keep coming back, and what can they now do independently?

Then we work on it, with clear examples, feedback and another opportunity to try.

That might mean modelling a sentence, helping the student build their own version, then returning to the activity so they can use it in context.

Make room for their ideas

Young people have plenty to say. Helping them find the English to say it—and the confidence to have a go—is a big part of why I enjoy teaching them.
