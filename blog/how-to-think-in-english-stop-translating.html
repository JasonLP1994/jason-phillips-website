How to think in English and stop translating in your head

Your English is buffering. Let’s give it a little less to load.

By Jason Phillips · 6 October 2026 · 5 min read

Your answer is ready. Unfortunately, the conversation has moved on.

You understood the question. You had something useful to say. Then you translated it, checked the grammar, changed a word and missed your moment. Marvellous. Your brain has opened a proofreading department.

If that sounds familiar, learning how to think in English can help. The aim is to make familiar English easier to use when you need it. You can start with a few ordinary sentences, rather than trying to narrate your entire life.

To translate less, practise useful phrases as complete units. Say them aloud in situations that matter to you, get feedback and use them again with small changes.

Why do you translate in your head?

Translation is a sensible tool when you are learning something new. The difficulty comes when you try to assemble every spoken sentence word by word. Natural English combinations may differ from the ones you use in your first language.

For example, we usually say “make a decision” and “take responsibility”. Learning each whole phrase gives you something useful to reach for in a conversation. You can explore more examples in my guide to common English collocation mistakes.

Of course, a pause does not always mean you are translating. Sometimes you need a word, a clearer idea or a moment to settle your nerves. That is why I want to hear you speak before deciding what we should practise.

Five habits to help you think in English

1. Start with thoughts you can already express

Use English for a small part of your day: “I need my keys.” “The train is late.” “I’ll call her after lunch.” Keep it manageable. Describing your morning coffee is a perfectly respectable start. It does not need a TED Talk.

2. Build a little bank of useful phrases

Choose expressions you regularly need, then learn how to finish them. These are sometimes called language chunks: words that commonly work together.

Explain an idea“What I mean is…”“What I mean is, we need more time.”

Start with two or three phrases you would actually say. An enormous vocabulary list is less appealing when you are trying to remember where you put your keys.

3. Move from thinking to speaking

Silent practice is useful, but spoken conversation also needs your voice. Tell me what you did yesterday, explain a problem at work or describe your weekend plans aloud. Use simple wording first. Add detail when you feel comfortable.

If you record yourself, listen for one thing: where did you get stuck? Write down a phrase that would help at that point and try again.

4. Listen for phrases, then reuse them

Choose a short English clip you can mostly understand. Notice one useful expression and how the speaker stresses it. Listen again, say it aloud, then put it into your own sentence.

“Could you talk me through that?” can become “Could you talk me through the new booking system?” You are practising both pronunciation and a real reason to use the phrase.

5. Repeat with a small change

After a correction, use the improved sentence again. Then change the subject, time or situation. “I made a decision yesterday” could become “We need to make a decision before Friday.”

That gives the phrase another job to do. Repetition can feel a little tedious, I know. But the moment a student catches their own mistake before I say anything? Lovely. That is progress I can actually hear.

A five minute English speaking exercise

  1. Choose one real question. For example, “What are you working on this week?”
  2. Pick two useful phrases. Try “At the moment…” and “The main thing I need to do is…”
  3. Answer aloud for about 30 seconds. Clear and simple is fine.
  4. Notice one sticking point. Look up a phrase or ask your teacher for a correction.
  5. Answer again with a different detail. Then revisit the question another day.

You can practise for five minutes without trying to fix your whole English vocabulary before breakfast.

Common questions about thinking in English

Is it wrong to translate when learning English?

No. Your first language can help you understand a new word or a complicated idea. The goal is to rely less on translating familiar phrases during conversation. You do not need to ban another language from your own head.

Can I practise English speaking on my own?

Yes. Short spoken answers and recordings are useful starting points. Make time for conversations too, because listening, responding and getting feedback are different from rehearsing alone.

How do I know I am improving?

Return to a familiar question and notice whether you can express the same idea more comfortably. Can you find the phrase you need? Can you correct yourself? Those are more useful checks than expecting every hesitation to disappear.
