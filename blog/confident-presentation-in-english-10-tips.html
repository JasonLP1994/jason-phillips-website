How to Give a Presentation in English with Confidence: 10 Practical Tips

Giving a presentation in English can feel much harder than having a normal conversation. The good news is that confidence comes from structure, preparation and realistic practice — not from speaking perfect English.

By Jason Phillips · 23 October 2026 · 11 min read

You may use English successfully every day and still feel nervous about presenting in it. A presentation removes many of the things that make normal conversation comfortable. More people are listening, the pressure feels higher, you have less time to organise your thoughts and you may have to explain complicated ideas clearly.

For professionals using English as an additional language, the challenge is rarely just vocabulary. You are managing content, timing, slides, pronunciation, audience expectations and nerves at the same time.

The solution is not to memorise a perfect script. A strong presentation is built around a clear message, a structure you can follow easily and language that feels natural enough to use under pressure.

These ten practical tips are designed to help you prepare a presentation in English that sounds clear, professional and confident.

1. Decide what your audience should remember

Before you write slides, decide what the presentation is actually for. Many presentations become difficult because the speaker tries to include everything they know instead of deciding what the audience needs.

Complete this sentence before you start:

After my presentation, I want the audience to understand / believe / do...

That sentence becomes your filter. If a statistic, story or slide does not support the main purpose, consider removing it. This makes your presentation easier to follow and reduces the amount of English you need to manage.

2. Use a structure you can remember without reading

A clear structure helps the audience, but it also helps the speaker. If you always know where you are in the presentation, you are less likely to panic when you forget an exact sentence.

A reliable structure is:

OpeningTopic, purpose and roadmap
Main point 1Idea + evidence or example
Main point 2Idea + evidence or example
Main point 3Idea + evidence or example
ConclusionKey message + next step

At the beginning, tell the audience what to expect. For example: “Today I'd like to focus on three areas: what has changed, why it matters and what I think we should do next.”

That one sentence gives your audience a map and gives you three clear points to return to.

3. Practise the first minute more than any other part

The beginning often feels hardest because your adrenaline is highest and you have not yet settled into the rhythm of speaking. If the opening feels automatic, the rest of the presentation usually becomes easier.

Practise your greeting, introduction and first transition aloud. Do not only read them silently. Your mouth needs to become familiar with the words.

Useful opening phrases include:

  • “Good morning. Thanks for joining me.”
  • “Today I'd like to talk about...”
  • “The purpose of this presentation is to...”
  • “I'll focus on three main points.”
  • “By the end, I hope you'll have a clearer picture of...”

Choose language that sounds like you. A simple phrase delivered confidently is better than an impressive phrase you are afraid of mispronouncing.

4. Use signposting language to guide the audience

In conversation, listeners can interrupt immediately if they are lost. During a presentation, they may stay silent. Signposting phrases show where one idea finishes and another begins.

Useful transitions include:

  • Moving on: “Let's move on to the second point.”
  • Highlighting: “The key point here is...”
  • Explaining: “What this means in practice is...”
  • Giving an example: “A good example of this is...”
  • Contrasting: “However, there is another side to this.”
  • Summarising: “So, what can we take from this?”

These expressions are valuable because they reduce the mental effort of connecting ideas. You do not need to invent a new transition each time; you can rely on a small set of phrases you know well.

5. Keep your English clear rather than unnecessarily complex

Professional English does not need to sound complicated. In fact, a presentation is usually stronger when sentences are shorter and the main idea arrives early.

Compare these two approaches:

Less clear“With regard to the implementation of the proposed strategy, it is our considered view that a phased approach would potentially be more beneficial.”

Clearer“We recommend introducing the strategy in stages.”

The second version is easier to say, easier to understand and easier to remember. If you are presenting technical material, explain specialist terms when necessary instead of assuming everyone understands them.

Useful clarification phrases are: “In simple terms...”, “In other words...” and “What this means for us is...”

6. Do not let the slides become your script

A slide should support the speaker, not replace them. If every sentence is written on screen, the audience has to choose between reading and listening.

Use slides for key numbers, diagrams, images, short phrases and information that genuinely benefits from being seen. Then explain the significance in your own words.

If you are worried about forgetting something, use presenter notes with keywords rather than complete paragraphs. Keywords encourage you to speak naturally while still giving you a safety net.

7. Slow down — especially when the information becomes important

Nervous speakers often accelerate. Unfortunately, speaking faster gives you less time to choose words, makes pronunciation less precise and gives the audience less time to process information.

Instead of trying to maintain one constant speed, deliberately slow down before an important point. Pause after a number, conclusion or recommendation.

For example:

“There is one figure I want you to remember...” [pause] “Forty-two per cent.”

A pause can feel very long to the person speaking, but to the audience it often sounds controlled and confident.

8. Rehearse difficult pronunciation before presentation day

You do not need a particular accent to give an excellent presentation. You do need to be understandable.

Before the presentation, identify the words you will say repeatedly: company names, technical terms, figures, names of places and key vocabulary. If one word keeps causing difficulty, practise it in the whole sentence where it will appear.

Also think about word stress. In English, stressing the important word can make a sentence much easier to understand: “We need to reduce the cost.” versus “We need to reduce the cost.”

Recording a short section of your presentation is one of the quickest ways to notice whether your pace, volume and pronunciation are helping or distracting from the message.

REHEARSE THE DIFFICULT BIT

What if someone interrupts?

You're presenting a proposal and someone asks, “Why are we spending more when the current system still works?” Rather than rushing into a defensive answer, buy yourself a moment: “That's a fair question. The main reason is…”

Notice what that gives you: a calm acknowledgement, a clear starting point and a chance to explain your evidence. Try rehearsing one uncomfortable question about your next presentation. Confidence isn't never getting stuck; it's knowing how to recover.

9. Prepare for questions instead of hoping there are none

The question-and-answer stage often worries non-native speakers more than the presentation itself because it cannot be scripted completely. You can still prepare for it.

Write down five to ten questions you think your audience may ask. Include one difficult question, one request for clarification and one question that challenges your conclusion. Then practise answering them aloud.

Useful Q&A phrases include:

  • “That's a good question.”
  • “If I understand you correctly, you're asking...”
  • “There are two parts to that.”
  • “Let me clarify what I mean.”
  • “I don't have that figure with me, but I can follow up afterwards.”
  • “That's outside the scope of today's presentation, but the short answer is...”

Notice that confident communication does not require knowing every answer. It requires being able to handle the interaction professionally.

10. Rehearse under conditions that feel like the real event

Silent preparation creates false confidence. You need to hear yourself say the presentation.

Stand up. Use the actual slides. Set a timer. Practise transitions. If possible, ask another person to interrupt with a question. The closer rehearsal feels to the real event, the more useful it becomes.

A good rehearsal sequence is:

Run 1Finish the presentation without stopping
ReviewChoose two areas to improve
Run 2Focus on those two areas
Q&AAnswer unexpected questions
Final runDeliver it naturally within the time limit

Trying to correct ten things at once is rarely effective. Choose the changes that will make the biggest difference to clarity and confidence.

Common presentation mistakes to avoid

Some problems appear repeatedly when professionals present in English. Reading full paragraphs from slides removes eye contact and makes delivery sound flat. Memorising every sentence creates panic when one line is forgotten. Using difficult vocabulary simply to sound professional can make ideas less clear. Speaking too quickly increases pronunciation problems. Apologising repeatedly for your English draws attention away from your expertise.

Your audience is usually more interested in your message than in analysing your grammar. Prepare carefully, but remember why you are there: you have information, experience or an idea worth sharing.

A simple five-minute presentation practice

If you want to improve your English presentation skills without preparing a full presentation, use this short exercise.

Choose a topic you know well. Spend one minute making a three-point plan. Speak for three minutes without reading a script. Then spend one minute answering an unexpected question.

Afterwards, review three things: Was your structure easy to follow? Which words or sentences were difficult to produce? Did you answer the question directly before adding detail?

Repeat the same presentation after receiving feedback. The second version will often reveal more improvement than practising five completely different topics.

Confidence comes from preparation you can trust

Giving a presentation in English is not about sounding like a native speaker or avoiding every mistake. It is about making your message easy to follow and staying in control when the situation becomes less predictable.

Clear structure reduces pressure. Familiar signposting phrases keep ideas connected. Realistic rehearsal builds automaticity. Question practice reduces fear of the unknown. Personalised feedback shows you where your communication can become sharper.

Most importantly, confidence grows when you have evidence that you can handle the task. Prepare in a way that gives you that evidence before presentation day.
