“You don’t do nothing” — one of the most common mistakes I hear in lessons.

The fix is simple: in standard English, if don’t already makes the sentence negative, we normally use anything, not nothing.

By Jason Phillips · British English Coach · Published 7 October 2026 · 5 min read
Instead ofYou don’t do nothing.
SayYou don’t do anything.
The quick rule:
If you already have don’t, doesn’t, didn’t, can’t, won’t or another negative verb, standard English normally uses anything / anyone / anywhere rather than nothing / nobody / nowhere.

The mistake: “You don’t do nothing”

I hear versions of this mistake regularly in lessons. A student wants to make the sentence stronger, so they use two negative words:

✗ You don’t do nothing.

✓ You don’t do anything.

The important part is that don’t has already made the sentence negative. In standard English, the second word is normally anything, not nothing.

✗ I don’t know nothing.

✓ I don’t know anything.

✗ She didn’t say nothing.

✓ She didn’t say anything.

✗ We can’t find nobody.

✓ We can’t find anybody.

Why learners make this mistake

For many learners, the mistake is completely logical. In a number of languages, two negative words can work together to express one negative meaning. The speaker is not trying to create a positive sentence; they are simply following a pattern that feels natural in their first language.

English also has dialects where this kind of negative concord is used naturally. You may hear native speakers say things such as “I didn’t see nothing” in informal speech, music, films or particular regional varieties of English.

That does not mean the speaker is confused. It means that dialect follows a different grammatical pattern.

But what should an English learner use?

If your goal is clear, widely accepted standard British or international English — especially for work, exams, interviews, presentations and professional communication — use one grammatical negative:

Negative verb + any-word

I don’t know anything.

She didn’t speak to anyone.

We can’t go anywhere.

No negative verb + no-word

I know nothing.

She spoke to nobody.

We can go nowhere.

Both columns can express a negative meaning. The difference is how the sentence is built.

Anything and nothing are not interchangeable

Compare these two sentences:

I don’t want anything.

I want nothing.

They can communicate a similar idea, but the second sentence sounds stronger, more absolute and sometimes more dramatic. In ordinary conversation, I don’t want anything is usually the more neutral choice.

The pattern to make automatic

  • don’t + anything — I don’t need anything.
  • doesn’t + anyone — He doesn’t know anyone here.
  • didn’t + anywhere — We didn’t go anywhere.
  • can’t + anything — I can’t hear anything.
  • won’t + anyone — She won’t tell anyone.

Once these combinations become automatic, you stop translating the sentence word by word and your English becomes quicker and more natural.

A common advanced-learner problem

This is also a good example of something I see with strong students: your vocabulary can be advanced while a small basic grammar habit keeps appearing underneath it.

Fluency is not only about learning harder words. It is also about making the most common structures automatic so you can use them correctly without stopping to think.

Quick practice

Choose the most natural standard-English option.

  1. I don’t need nothing / anything.
  2. She didn’t tell nobody / anybody.
  3. We can’t find nowhere / anywhere to park.
  4. He knows nothing / anything about the problem.
  5. I haven’t heard nothing / anything yet.

Answers: anything · anybody · anywhere · nothing · anything

The takeaway

✗ You don’t do nothing.

✓ You don’t do anything.

Don’t is already negative, so the sentence normally continues with an any- word. It is a tiny correction, but these are exactly the changes that make spoken English sound cleaner, more confident and more natural.
