Professional English guide

English job interview questions and answers for professionals.

Prepare the language, structure and examples you need to answer clearly under pressure — without sounding memorised.

Strong interview English is not about using the most complicated vocabulary possible. It is about answering the question directly, organising your ideas, giving evidence and sounding calm enough for the interviewer to follow you.

A reliable interview answer structure:
1. Answer the question directly.
2. Add the relevant context.
3. Give one specific example.
4. Explain the result or impact.
5. Connect it back to the role.

1. “Tell me about yourself.”

This is usually an invitation to give a professional summary, not your life story. Focus on your current role, relevant experience, strongest area of expertise and what you are looking for next.

Useful structure: “I’m currently working as… Over the last X years, I’ve focused mainly on… One area I’ve developed particularly strongly is… I’m now looking for a role where I can…”

Example: “I’m currently working in operations, where I coordinate projects across several European markets. Over the last six years, I’ve developed strong experience in process improvement and stakeholder communication. I’m now looking for a role where I can take more ownership of strategic projects and work more closely with senior decision-makers.”

2. “Why do you want this role?”

Avoid generic answers such as “It is a great company.” Show a connection between the role, your experience and the next step you want in your career.

“What attracted me to the role is…” · “The part that particularly interests me is…” · “It builds naturally on my experience in…” · “I think I could add value by…”

3. “What are your strengths?”

Name a strength that matters for the role, then prove it. The example is what makes the answer convincing.

“One of my main strengths is my ability to…” · “A good example of that was when…” · “The result was…”

4. “What is a weakness you are working on?”

Choose a real but manageable weakness. Explain what you are doing about it. Avoid answers that are obviously disguised strengths.

Example: “Earlier in my career, I sometimes spent too long perfecting details before sharing work. I’ve become much better at agreeing the level of detail needed at the start and sharing earlier drafts, which has made me faster and more collaborative.”

5. Competency questions: use STAR

Questions beginning with “Tell me about a time when…” need evidence. STAR keeps the answer organised.

  • Situation: What was happening?
  • Task: What were you responsible for?
  • Action: What did you personally do?
  • Result: What changed because of your action?
Language for STAR:
Situation: “At the time, we were…”
Task: “My responsibility was to…”
Action: “The first thing I did was…” / “I decided to…”
Result: “As a result…” / “This meant that…” / “We were able to…”

6. “Tell me about a difficult situation.”

Do not spend most of the answer describing the problem. Interviewers care about how you responded.

“The main challenge was…” · “I realised that we needed to…” · “I spoke with…” · “I proposed…” · “In the end…”

7. “Tell me about a conflict at work.”

Keep the tone professional. Avoid blaming the other person. Show that you can clarify expectations, listen and move towards a solution.

Useful language: “We had different views on…” · “I wanted to understand their concern first…” · “We agreed on…” · “That helped us move forward without delaying the project.”

8. “Why are you leaving your current job?”

Keep this forward-looking. Even if the real situation is frustrating, focus on what you want next.

Better: “I’ve learned a lot in my current role, but I’m ready for more responsibility and a role with greater exposure to international projects.”

9. “Why should we hire you?”

This is your chance to summarise fit. Choose two or three relevant strengths and link them directly to the position.

“I think I bring three things that are particularly relevant…” · “First…” · “Second…” · “Finally…” · “That combination would allow me to…”

10. “Do you have any questions for us?”

Prepare questions that help you understand the role and also show how you think.

  • “What would success look like in the first six months?”
  • “What are the biggest challenges facing the team at the moment?”
  • “How does this role interact with other departments?”
  • “What distinguishes people who perform particularly well in this team?”

How to sound more confident in English

Confidence is often more about delivery than vocabulary. Slow down slightly, finish one idea before starting the next, and use signposting to make your structure obvious.

“There are two main reasons…” · “The first point I’d make is…” · “A good example would be…” · “What I learned from that was…” · “If I were in that situation again…”

What to avoid

  • Memorising full answers word for word.
  • Using very long sentences when you are nervous.
  • Giving background without answering the actual question.
  • Using advanced vocabulary you cannot control naturally.
  • Saying “we” throughout a competency answer without explaining what you did.

A simple practice method

  1. Choose five likely questions.
  2. Write only bullet points, not a full script.
  3. Record a 60–90 second answer.
  4. Listen for unclear structure, repetition and grammar errors.
  5. Repeat the answer with fewer words and clearer signposting.
