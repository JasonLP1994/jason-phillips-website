IELTS Speaking questions for Parts 1, 2 and 3 — with sample answers

The IELTS Speaking test checks how well you communicate your own ideas, not how many memorised expressions you can repeat. Practise these realistic sample questions to develop natural answers for all three parts.

By Jason Phillips · British English Coach · 25/11/2026 · Free learning guide

How is IELTS Speaking organised? The speaking test normally lasts 11–14 minutes. Part 1 covers familiar questions, Part 2 asks you to speak at length on a topic, and Part 3 develops a more abstract discussion related to Part 2. The questions below are original practice examples, not predictions of the questions you will receive.

IELTS Speaking Part 1: familiar questions

Part 1 is an introduction and interview. You may be asked about your home, work, studies, hobbies or everyday routines. Give a direct answer, then add one relevant detail. You do not need a speech for every question.

1. Do you enjoy the area where you live?

Sample answer: “Yes, mostly because it's within walking distance of the town centre. It can get busy at weekends, but there's a park nearby where I go to relax.”

Why it works: It answers directly, gives a reason and includes a natural contrast.

2. How often do you cook?

Sample answer: “I usually cook at home during the week. I enjoy trying new recipes, although I tend to make something quick when I'm working late.”

3. Do you prefer working alone or with other people?

Sample answer: “It depends on the task. If I'm writing a report, I prefer working independently, but brainstorming is usually better with a team.”

Try these three questions without reading an answer: What do you normally do at weekends? Have your interests changed since you were younger? Is there a skill you would like to learn? Record yourself and notice where you repeat simple words or pause because an idea is unclear.

IELTS Speaking Part 2: the long turn

You receive a topic card and have one minute to prepare. You then speak for up to two minutes. Short notes are useful, but writing a full script in the preparation minute is not realistic.

Practice cue card

Describe a place you visited that made a strong impression on you.

You should say where it was, when you visited, what you did there and explain why you remember it so clearly.

Sample response

“I'd like to talk about a small coastal town I visited a few years ago. I went there with two friends for a long weekend, mainly because we wanted a break from the city. We spent most of our time walking along the seafront and trying local food. What surprised me most was how relaxed the place felt, even though there were quite a few visitors. On the second evening, we found a quiet viewpoint just before sunset. We stayed there for nearly an hour, talking and watching the light change. I still remember that moment because I hadn't realised how much I needed a slower pace. Since that trip, I've tried to make more time for short breaks, even when work is busy.”

This is a short model response, not a guaranteed band-score answer. To extend it naturally, add one specific incident, a comparison with another place or a reflection on how your feelings changed.

IELTS Speaking Part 3: discuss ideas in more depth

Part 3 moves from your personal experience towards broader ideas. A useful structure is opinion → reason → example or comparison → qualification. It helps you develop a thought without sounding like you're reciting a formula.

Why do people enjoy travelling to unfamiliar places?

Sample answer: “One reason is that travelling gives people a break from routine. It can also challenge their assumptions about how other people live. For example, someone who has only lived in a large city might discover that a slower lifestyle suits them. Of course, not everyone enjoys uncertainty, so I think it depends on personality as well.”

Has technology changed the way people experience travel?

Sample answer: “Definitely. Online maps and translation tools make unfamiliar places easier to navigate, which can encourage more independent travel. On the other hand, constantly checking a phone can distract people from the experience itself. I think technology is most useful when it supports the trip rather than controls it.”

Other questions to practise: Should cities limit the number of tourists? What can people learn by living abroad? Will virtual tourism become more popular?

What do IELTS examiners assess?

IELTS Speaking uses four criteria: fluency and coherence, lexical resource, grammatical range and accuracy, and pronunciation. Speaking quickly or using difficult words does not automatically improve your score. Clear, developed ideas and consistent control matter.

A 15-minute preparation routine

  1. 3 minutes: answer three Part 1 questions naturally.
  2. 4 minutes: prepare for one Part 2 topic for one minute, speak for two, then review the recording.
  3. 5 minutes: answer two or three related Part 3 questions with reasons and examples.
  4. 3 minutes: repeat one answer, improving only your two biggest weaknesses.

Avoid memorising complete answers. You might receive a different question, and heavily rehearsed responses can sound unnatural or fail to address what was asked.
