How to pronounce TH in British English: two sounds and common mistakes

English TH is not one sound. Compare think /θɪŋk/ with this /ðɪs/: the tongue position is similar, but the voice works differently. Here's how to practise both sounds without trying to erase your accent.

By Jason Phillips · British English Coach · 27/11/2026 · Free learning guide

TH has two common sounds in English: /θ/ as in think and /ð/ as in this. Both are dental fricatives: air passes through a small space between the tongue and teeth. The difference is that /θ/ is voiceless and /ð/ is voiced.

Sound 1: /θ/ — think, three, Thursday

To produce /θ/, bring the tip of your tongue gently towards your upper front teeth. It can touch the teeth or sit very slightly between them. Blow air steadily through the gap without using your voice. Do not bite your tongue or push it out excessively.

Try these words slowly: think, thank, three, thick, both, healthy, maths, Thursday. Place your fingers lightly on your throat. For a clear isolated /θ/, you should not feel strong vocal vibration.

Practice sentence: “I think three things are worth discussing on Thursday.”

Start slowly. The aim is a consistent /θ/, not speed.

Sound 2: /ð/ — this, that, they

Keep nearly the same tongue position, but now let your vocal cords vibrate. This creates /ð/, the sound at the beginning of this, that and they. You should feel vibration in your throat while producing it.

Practise: this, these, that, those, they, them, together, weather, mother.

Practice sentence: “They said that these ideas were better than the others.”

In natural connected speech, very common words such as the, this and that often appear without a long, strongly pronounced TH sound. Begin clearly, then practise at conversational speed.

Why TH can sound like T, D, S, Z or F

Different languages use different sound systems. If TH is unfamiliar in yours, your mouth may substitute a more familiar sound. That's a normal starting point for pronunciation practice.

  • TH becomes /t/: three may sound like tree. Make the airflow continuous for /θ/ instead of stopping it.
  • TH becomes /d/: they may sound like day. Keep air moving instead of making a complete stop.
  • TH becomes /s/ or /z/: move the tongue forwards towards the teeth rather than leaving it behind them.
  • TH becomes /f/: avoid placing the upper teeth against the lower lip; the tongue should create the friction.

Minimal pairs: train your ears as well as your mouth

Some of these word pairs vary slightly by accent, but they are useful for hearing the target contrast. Listen first, then repeat and record yourself:

TH wordOther wordDifference
thin /θɪn/tin /tɪn/continuous air vs a stop
three /θriː/tree /triː//θ/ vs /t/
then /ðen/den /den//ð/ vs /d/
think /θɪŋk/sink /sɪŋk/dental vs alveolar friction
though /ðəʊ/dough /dəʊ//ð/ vs /d/

British English note: Pronunciation varies across the UK. Some accents naturally use different consonants in words such as think or this. The /θ/ and /ð/ targets above are particularly useful for learners aiming towards widely taught standard southern British pronunciation, but accent variation is not inherently incorrect.

Five sentences to practise

  1. “Three thoughtful colleagues thanked the team.”
  2. “This Thursday, they're meeting their new manager.”
  3. “I thought the third option was worth considering.”
  4. “Those are the things that matter most.”
  5. “Although the weather changed, they enjoyed the walk.”

Record each sentence twice. First, exaggerate the target sounds slightly so you can feel them. Then repeat at a normal pace. Notice whether your tongue moves to the lips for /f/ or whether a stop appears where you want continuous air.

A five-minute TH pronunciation routine

  1. One minute: alternate /θ/ and /ð/ while checking throat vibration.
  2. One minute: repeat five individual words with each sound.
  3. One minute: practise three minimal pairs.
  4. One minute: say two complete sentences naturally.
  5. One minute: record and replay one sentence, correcting only the clearest issue.
